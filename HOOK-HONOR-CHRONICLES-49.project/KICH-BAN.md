## HOOK

Otis Tate knelt beside the shivering old woman and held out his lunch box.

A fist cracked into his shoulder from behind.

Wade Prescott ripped the boy off the bench and slammed him face-first into wet pavement. "What are you doing to her?" He towered over Otis, phone aimed at the boy's face. "Black kid crouching over a helpless old white woman at midnight — I'm calling 911 right now."

"I was feeding her, sir." Otis's cheek pressed against cold concrete.

The lunch box lay tipped between them. His only food in two days. His stomach clenched. His mother had nothing at home either. But those shaking hands. Those blue lips.

Otis crawled past Wade, picked up the box, and pressed it into the old woman's fingers. "Eat, ma'am. Please."

Sixteen. Starving. He just gave the last food he owned to a stranger he'd never see again. Except he would. And nothing in his life would stay the same.

Have you ever given away the last thing you had, not knowing if tomorrow would be kind enough to replace it — because you're about to meet someone who did exactly that, drop a comment if you've been there, hit that like, and let me take you back to where it all started.

## PHẦN SAU HOOK (chỉ dùng lấy ngữ cảnh & nhân vật chuẩn — KHÔNG dựng shot)

Six hours earlier, Otis Tate had clocked in at Ridgecrest Tower with mud still caked on his sneakers. The lobby gleamed under fluorescent strips. Gerald Whitmore stood behind the front desk, arms crossed, watching Otis push the mop across imported marble.

"Missed a spot." Gerald pointed at the southeast corner without looking up. "Start over."

Otis dragged the bucket back. He mopped the same stretch a second time, wringing gray water through cracked fingers. The mop head was splitting at the seams. He had asked for a replacement three weeks ago. Gerald said it came out of the maintenance budget and the maintenance budget was spent.

"Still streaking." Gerald sipped coffee. "Third time. Whole floor."

Otis did not argue. He started at the entrance and worked toward the elevators again, pressing harder, keeping his breathing even. A tenant in a cashmere coat stepped through the freshly mopped section without pausing. Wet footprints trailed behind her heels. Gerald glanced at them, then at Otis.

"Clean that too."

Forty minutes. Three full passes. By the time Gerald nodded, Otis's lower back burned in a straight line from hip to hip. He wheeled the bucket to the supply closet and emptied it in the utility sink. Denise Carter was already inside, restocking paper towels on the metal shelf.

"He made you redo the lobby again?" She handed him a water bottle without being asked.

"Three times." Otis drank half of it in one pull.

Denise shook her head. She was twenty-nine, a single mother of two, and the only person in the building who treated Otis like a human being rather than a piece of equipment. She had trained him on his first day — showed him which chemicals not to mix, which tenants tipped, which stairwell doors jammed. When Gerald screamed, Denise translated it into something survivable.

"You eat today?" she asked.

"I will." He looked away because the answer was no.

Denise pulled a granola bar from her apron and set it on the shelf beside his elbow. "For later," she said, and left before he could refuse.

Otis finished his shift at ten. He opened his locker in the basement. Inside: a blue lunch box, a water bottle, a math textbook with a broken spine, and a photograph taped to the door. His mother, Ruth, smiling from a hospital bed two years ago, before the second surgery failed and the wheelchair became permanent. He touched the photo once, closed the locker, and pulled on his jacket.

The lunch box held rice, black beans, and a piece of chicken he had cooked at four that morning. He had divided the pot in half — one portion for Ruth, one for himself. There was nothing else in the refrigerator. He had checked.

Otis walked to the service exit and pushed through the heavy door into November air. Rain had started sometime during his shift, steady and cold, the kind that turned Nashville gutters into shallow rivers. He had no umbrella. He zipped his jacket to his throat and started walking.

Forty minutes. That was the distance between Ridgecrest Tower and the apartment on Shelby Avenue where Ruth waited. He had calculated it once — two miles and three hundred feet, if he cut through Riverfront Park. The bus cost a dollar seventy-five. He had stopped taking it in September when the grocery bill climbed past what his paycheck could cover.

His sneakers soaked through in the first block. Rain ran down his neck and pooled at his collar. He tucked the lunch box inside his jacket, pressing it against his ribs to keep the food dry. That meal was the only thing standing between his mother and another night of nothing.

He did the math while he walked, the way he always did. Paycheck: three hundred eighty dollars every two weeks. Rent: two-ten. Electric: thirty-eight. Ruth's medication copay: forty-two. Phone — the prepaid one, the cheapest plan — fifteen. Rice, beans, chicken thighs, eggs, bread: whatever remained. Last week, whatever remained was forty-five dollars.

He was sixteen years old. He had dropped out of Maplewood High four months ago, the week Ruth fell from the wheelchair and cracked two ribs because the wheel lock had rusted through. The chair itself had been broken for four months. Medicaid said it was processing. Otis called every Tuesday. The answer was always the same.

The rain hit harder. He passed a lit bar on Second Avenue. Through the window he saw Wade Prescott pouring bourbon for a man in a leather jacket. Wade laughed. Otis kept walking.

Riverfront Park was dark. The lampposts along the east path had been out since August. Otis knew every cracked step, every root that buckled the concrete. He had memorized the route with his feet.

That was when he saw her.

A shape on the bench near the river wall. Small. Hunched. Shaking under a coat so thin it looked like paper. White hair caught the distant glow of a streetlight one block north. Bare ankles above ruined shoes.

An old woman, alone, in freezing rain, at close to midnight.

Otis's hand tightened around the lunch box beneath his jacket.

Otis knelt beside the bench. Up close, the woman was smaller than he had guessed — thin wrists, hollowed cheeks, skin so pale it almost glowed in the dark. Her fingers curled around the edge of the bench slats like she was afraid the wind might lift her away.

"Ma'am?" He kept his voice low. "Are you hurt?"

She turned her head. Her eyes found him but did not quite focus. "Henry?" She reached toward his face. "Henry, you came back."

"I'm not Henry, ma'am." Otis unzipped his jacket. Rain hammered his shoulders immediately. "I'm Otis. I work at the building down the road."

"Henry always brings the tea." Her voice was a whisper wrapped in gravel. "He never forgets the tea."

She was confused. Lost, maybe. Alzheimer's or dementia — Otis had seen it in Mrs. Patterson on the third floor of his apartment building before she was moved to a facility last spring. The same drifting gaze. The same hands searching for someone who was not there.

He looked around the park. Empty paths. No cars on the road beyond the wall. No one was coming for this woman. Not Henry. Not anyone.

Rain pooled in the creases of her coat. Her lips had turned the color of a bruise. She was shivering in short, violent bursts, the kind that meant the body was running out of ways to stay warm.

Otis thought about Ruth. Right now his mother was lying in a bed with a blanket that was too thin because the heating bill was three months past due and they kept the thermostat at fifty-eight to survive. She was waiting for this lunch box. She had eaten a piece of bread at noon and nothing since.

He opened the box. Steam rose faintly from the rice. The chicken was still warm — he had packed it in foil and tucked it tight. The smell hit his empty stomach like a fist.

He could walk away. He could close the lid, zip his jacket, and cover those forty minutes home. Ruth would eat. He would eat tomorrow, maybe. That was the plan.

But this woman's hands were shaking so hard she could not grip the bench anymore. Her breathing came in thin, ragged pulls, each one shorter than the last.

Then the footsteps came.

Heavy boots on wet pavement, fast and deliberate. Wade Prescott appeared from the direction of Second Avenue, collar turned up against the rain, phone already glowing in his hand. He must have seen Otis through the park entrance from his bar.

"Hey!" Wade's voice cracked through the rain. "What are you doing to that woman?"

Otis stood. "She's freezing, sir. I'm trying to help her."

"Help her?" Wade closed the distance in four strides. He was six-two, thick through the shoulders, and the kind of man who filled a doorway on purpose. "A Black kid crouching over an old white woman in the dark? That's not help. That's a crime."

"I'm giving her food."

"Put the box down. Step away from her. Right now." Wade raised his phone. "I'm recording this. I'm calling 911. You don't move."

The old woman whimpered. She reached for Otis's arm. "Henry, don't leave."

"I'm not leaving, ma'am."

"I said step away." Wade pushed Otis backward with one hand. The boy's sneakers slid on wet grass and he fell, catching himself on his palms. The lunch box tumbled onto the ground between them. Rice scattered across dark soil.

Otis's chest heaved. He stared at the spilled rice. That was dinner. That was Ruth's dinner. Hours of standing over a hot stove at four in the morning, measuring every grain because there was nothing to waste.

Gone. Scattered in the mud.

Something cracked inside him — not anger, not surrender. Something quieter. A decision that did not care about consequences.

He picked himself up. He gathered what remained in the box — beans, chicken, a handful of rice still clinging to the foil. He wiped the lid on his shirt. He turned his back on Wade Prescott, knelt beside the old woman, and pressed the box into her trembling hands.

"Eat, ma'am. Please."

Wade grabbed his shoulder. "I told you — "

"Then call the police." Otis did not turn around. "Call them. But she eats first."

The old woman's fingers closed around the box. She lifted a spoonful of rice to her lips. Her shaking slowed, just slightly. Just enough.

Otis stayed on his knees in the rain, a forty-five-year-old man's grip digging into his shoulder, and watched a stranger eat the last meal his family owned.

Somewhere behind them, Wade's phone was already dialing.

And somewhere across Nashville, in a penthouse on West End Avenue, a man named Harrison Ashworth was about to learn that his grandmother had been missing for six hours.
