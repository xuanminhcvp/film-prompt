## PHẦN CÒN LẠI CỦA KỊCH BẢN (chưa dựng — chỉ để tham chiếu ngữ cảnh)

Her mother, Marva Tillman, had kept house for Emmett Arceneaux for sixteen years. Not the CEO you just heard laughing — the founder. The original. The quiet one. Emmett built Arclight Systems out of a garage into the firm that guarded other people's fortunes, and he trusted exactly one person to move through his home without performing for him, and that person came every day at seven with her cleaning caddy and, in the summers, a small daughter who did her homework at the kitchen island.

The games started when Celeste was eight. She'd found a slip of paper under the fruit bowl — five-letter groups in a careful hand, nonsense to anyone normal. It took her three days with a library book to realize the nonsense was a sentence wearing a disguise. The sentence was: CHECK THE COOKIE JAR. There were two dollars in the cookie jar.

After that, there was always something under the fruit bowl.

He never simplified them because she was a child. That was the thing she loved him for before she had words for it. The ciphers grew as she grew — substitution, then keyword, then the hard ones — and they had one standing rule, their whole architecture in a sentence: the key is always the first line of the last book I lent you. He lent her books constantly. Puzzle books, history books, a battered paperback on the codebreakers of the last century written by a woman named Dr. Ingrid Halloran. Celeste couldn't afford her own copy of the famous one, the eighty-dollar textbook everyone in that world swore by — so at fourteen she sat in the library three afternoons a week and copied whole chapters by hand into a marbled composition notebook, diagrams and all. Cheapest education in America: a two-dollar notebook and the patience to fill it.

At sixteen, under the handle Glasswing, she entered a national high-school codebreaking championship and won it — a nobody from nowhere beating magnet-school teams with corporate coaches. The trophy went on the TV stand. The scholarship offers went in a drawer, because the competition's anonymous sponsor covered travel, not tuition, and eighteen arrived the same year her mother's knee finally gave out on the Arceneaux staircase. Surgery, then rehab, then bills. Celeste took the full ride out of the drawer, looked at it for a long minute, and enrolled at the community college twenty minutes from home instead. Second year now. Library job on weekends. Nobody at Arclight knew her name.

One man did. Emmett used to follow the championship scoreboards the way other men followed baseball — and if his eyes went bright whenever the kitchen kid explained a cipher back to him better than he'd built it, he never made a speech about it. He just kept lending books.

Three weeks ago, Emmett Arceneaux sat down in his study after dinner and didn't stand up again. Aneurysm. Sixty-six. The obituaries called him a titan and got his company's name right and his life entirely wrong. Marva cleaned the study herself before the family arrived, because no stranger was going to be the one to move his chair.

And four days before he died — this is the part that wouldn't stop itching — a package had arrived at the Tillman apartment. Brown paper, no note, no card. A first-edition puzzle anthology, the exact kind he always chose, the kind with a first line you could set your watch by. Celeste had texted him a thank-you and gotten back a single line: Last one for a while. Read the beginning carefully.

She'd thought he meant the first chapter.

She put the book on the shelf, and grief put a film over everything, and for three weeks the strangeness of it sat in the back of her mind like a pebble in a shoe — until a Saturday morning when her mother's knee buckled on the bus steps, and Celeste rode along to the Arceneaux estate to carry trays she wasn't supposed to carry, into a war room she wasn't supposed to enter, where nine strangers in expensive exhaustion were losing a fight against five lines of handwriting she would have recognized from across a football field.

The last book he ever lent her was sitting on her shelf at home.

The first line was already in her memory, where he'd known she would keep it.

Here is what five lines of handwriting can do to a half-billion dollars.

Arclight Systems held custody of client assets — retirement funds, escrow accounts, settlement trusts — and the crown of the whole operation was a cold-storage vault holding five hundred million dollars in bearer instruments and digital assets. Air-gapped machine, military-grade encryption, one master passphrase. And through a spectacular piece of governance that seemed charming right up until it became a catastrophe, the only human being who held that passphrase was the founder.

Emmett Arceneaux had his reasons. Years ago, a breach traced back to a shared credential had nearly ended the company, and Emmett — brilliant, stubborn, allergic to committees — concluded that a secret two people know is not a secret. The board scheduled a dual-key ceremony to fix it. It was postponed. Twice. Both times, the postponement memo carried the signature of the chief technology officer, Gideon Falk, who had budgets to protect and considered the old man's paranoia a line item.

Then the old man sat down in his study one night and didn't stand up.

What he left, in the fireproof envelope his lawyers opened eleven days before the deadline that could kill the company, was not a passphrase. It was a single card, five lines of letter groups in his careful hand, and beneath them, one printed sentence:

For the one who checks under the fruit bowl.

The lawyers thought it was dementia they'd somehow missed. The board thought it was a prank from beyond the grave. Gideon Falk called it the founder's nonsense poem, and hired people to go around it.

They tried. Kestrel Group — nine days at the estate, a forensic team led by a silver consultant named Perry Sloane who billed like a surgeon and spoke of quantum rigs in the reverent tones of a man selling them. And, at the insistence of the outside counsel, one more hire: Dr. Ingrid Halloran, sixty-six, retired from the kind of government service you don't put on a business card, author of the field's standard textbook. Halloran took one look at the card, said something quiet that nobody wanted to hear, and spent the next nine days being ignored by men with more expensive laptops.

The math was not negotiable. The vault's encryption could not be brute-forced before the sun burned out. The passphrase could not be guessed. The recovery deadline — Monday, nine a.m., when Meridian Capital's acquisition either closed or collapsed and five hundred million in client assets either moved or froze into lawsuits — could not be extended.

Hollis Drummond, the CEO who had spent a decade being the loud half of Emmett's quiet, moved the war room into the dead man's own glass conservatory, because the air-gapped machine lived at the estate and could not leave. Which is how, on the ninth day, a Saturday, the crisis acquired a coffee problem, and the coffee problem acquired a housekeeper, and the housekeeper's knee gave out on the bus steps at 6:40 that morning.

Marva Tillman had worked that house sixteen years and would no more call in sick during a crisis than burn it down. So her daughter came to carry the trays.

Security stopped Celeste at the service door. She was expected — her mother had cleared it — but the weekend guard looked at the twenty-year-old Black girl in a second-hand library polo and decided expected didn't apply to people who looked like her. He made her wait nineteen minutes on the gravel while he found someone to vouch for what a phone call had already vouched for. Celeste waited. She'd been waiting on doors like him her whole life. Her mother needed the trays carried; that outranked her pride.

By ten, she'd learned the rhythm of the room — you're invisible in an apron, and men talk over invisible people. Falk pacing, phone glued on. Sloane's team feeding candidate phrases into a rig with the effect of rice thrown at a bank vault. Drummond performing calm for a video call with Meridian. And projected on the big screen, eight feet tall, the scan of a card she could have described with her eyes closed.

Five-letter groups. That handwriting. And a double Q sitting in the third line — the null he always dropped in, the wink, the fingerprint of a game played across a fruit bowl for twelve years.

Celeste stood in a dead man's conservatory with a coffee pot going cold in her hand and understood, all at once, three things. The card was not a poem. The card was not for these people. And the strange, silent package on her shelf at home — Last one for a while. Read the beginning carefully — had not been a book recommendation.

She should have set down the pot and said nothing. Ask anyone who's ever been twenty and broke and Black in a room full of expensive tempers: you don't volunteer. But Perry Sloane chose that moment to announce, with a rich man's sigh, that the string was cryptographically dead — random groups, no structure, a grief artifact, and Falk seconded it, nonsense poem, and something in Celeste that had been raised by a librarian's patience and a founder's fruit bowl simply refused to let the sentence stand.

That's not nonsense, she said. Those groups aren't random. There's a null digraph in line three. He put it there on purpose.

The room turned. It was a room discovering that a piece of furniture had opinions.

Falk recovered first. The help is doing cryptanalysis now, he said, to laughter with Sloane's whole bench in it. Sweetheart, this is a little past dusting.

She's my daughter, Marva said from the doorway, and she's —

It's fine, Mama. Celeste set the pot down, level, unhurried. Line one and line four share a repeated trigraph at matching offsets, she said, to no one in particular, which you'd catch if anyone here had looked at it as handwriting instead of scanning it as data. He wrote this for a person, not a machine.

Dr. Ingrid Halloran sat up slowly, like a woman hearing her own language in a foreign airport.

But Hollis Drummond got there first, and Hollis Drummond had been marinating in nine days of terror, and terror in men like him curdles into theater. He crossed the conservatory with his phone already out, grinning the grin of a man who has found, at last, someone beneath him.

You know what, he said. I've paid nine experts for nine days of nothing. Why not the maid's kid. He thumbed the stopwatch open, held the screen up for the room, for the recording he'd been keeping for the board, for history. Five hundred million dollars, sweetheart. That's what's locked behind that screen. Crack it, and I'll pay you every cent. You've got two minutes.
