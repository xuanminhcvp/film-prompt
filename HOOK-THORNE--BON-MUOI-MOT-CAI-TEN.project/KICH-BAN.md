## KỊCH BẢN

"Whoever you are, you're nobody here. Security — walk this outlet shopper out."

The floor manager tore the plain business card in half without reading it. Dropped both pieces on the marble.

The Black man in the worn canvas jacket didn't move. Around him, the staff of the city's most expensive store smirked in formation.

"One minute," Felix Thorne said quietly. "First — introductions."

He looked at the floor manager.

"Sterling Vance. Floor manager. Eleven years. Three buried customer complaints."

The smirk flickered.

"Josh Pemberton. Senior associate, six years. Paige Dunmore, two years — currently posting me on her story."

Phones lowered, one by one. Nobody had a name tag with any of that on it.

"How—" Vance's mouth went dry. "How do you know our—"

At the bottom of the escalator, the store director rounded the corner, saw the man in the cheap jacket, and stopped breathing.

"Mr. Thorne?!"

Have you ever watched an entire room realize — at the same second — that they just humiliated the man holding all their careers in his memory, because you are about to, so drop a comment below, smash that like, and let me take you back to where it started.

Hargrove and Sons had been dressing this city's money for ninety years, and for the last six of them, it had been dying in slow motion.

Flagship sales down. Younger customers gone. And a number on the internal dashboards that the board couldn't stop staring at: customer dignity scores — the survey line that asks did we treat you well — sitting at the bottom of the entire luxury sector.

People didn't stop shopping at Hargrove because of the prices. They stopped because of how it felt to walk in.

So the board did the desperate thing. They went and got The Janitor.

That was the nickname the business press used, with respect. Felix Thorne, fifty-nine, the man companies called when they were six quarters from the grave. Two department-store chains, a hotel group, and a famous shoe brand all owed him their pulse. He was known for three things: turnarounds that held, an allergy to cameras — there were maybe four usable photos of him in existence, all blurry — and one leadership habit so famous it had been written up in business schools.

Before his first day, at every company, Felix Thorne memorized names.

Not the org chart. Names. Every employee in whatever building he was about to walk into — files, tenure, commendations, complaints, the shape of their record. He'd learned it from his father and polished it into doctrine: You can't lead people whose names you won't learn. At his last company, he'd stunned a warehouse crew by greeting all of them, first and last, on day one. It wasn't a trick to him. It was the job.

He'd taken the Hargrove job for a reason he told no one. His father had sewn for a Hargrove buyer once, decades back — piecework, beautiful, uncredited — and the old man used to stop at these same brass doors on Saturday walks and study the window suits the way other men study scripture. He never went in. Not once in thirty years. When Felix asked why, Marcus Thorne had said, without bitterness, because I already know what happens in there to men dressed like me, son. I'd rather love the windows.

A month ago, a headhunter had slid the Hargrove file across a table. Felix had signed before the coffee came.

The Hargrove announcement was set for Monday. Internal memo only — a name, no photograph, because Felix never allowed photographs. Which meant that for one last weekend, the incoming chief executive of Hargrove and Sons was the most powerful anonymous man in the city.

He spent it the way he always spent that weekend. Undercover, in his own stores.

Saturday morning, he prepared for the flagship the way other executives prepare for board meetings. He read all forty-one personnel files — the flagship ran on forty-one people — twice, over coffee, committing them the way he'd once committed fabric weights and thread counts. Sterling Vance, floor manager, eleven years, three customer complaints quietly zeroed out by a friendly director. Josh Pemberton, senior associate, six years, top commissions, a note about "steering" walk-ins he judged unpromising. Paige Dunmore, two years. A young cashier named Dominique Wells, ten months, two customer letters praising her by name — the only two such letters the flagship had received all year.

Forty-one names. He knew them cold by midnight.

Then he laid out his clothes for the morning: plain trousers, plain shirt, and the jacket.

The jacket was the one thing in Felix Thorne's closet that money couldn't reproduce. Heavy canvas, faded at the cuffs, patched once at the elbow with stitching so fine you'd need a loupe to find it. His father had cut and sewn it himself in 1968 — a tailor from Alabama who'd moved north with a machine and a gift, and spent thirty years making beautiful clothes for men who never once let him through their front doors. Marcus Thorne had died with strong hands and no store of his own.

Felix wore the jacket every year, on the first undercover walk, at every company. His people assumed the getup was strategy — dress poor, test the staff.

It was never strategy. It was his father's work. It was the finest garment he owned, by the only measure his father had taught him to trust — the seams.

If a store couldn't see the man in that jacket, it couldn't see anybody.

Saturday, 10:12 AM, Felix Thorne pushed open the brass doors of the Hargrove flagship, in his father's jacket, with forty-one names in his head.

The marble hall smelled like cedar and money. Somewhere to his left, a floor manager looked up, read the jacket in half a second, and started walking over with a smile that had nothing good in it.

Saturday at the flagship began the way it had for ninety years — brass doors, cedar air, the pianist unpacking sheet music by the atrium — and none of the forty-one people on shift had any idea they were about to spend the morning auditioning for their own jobs.

Sterling Vance had a gift, and the gift was arithmetic.

Eleven years on the Hargrove floor had taught him to price a customer at the door — shoes, watch, haircut, skin — and route them accordingly. The math took him under a second, and he trusted it the way pilots trust instruments. He called it reading the client. The three complaints buried in his file called it other things.

The floor he ran was beautiful, and the beauty was the alibi. Cedar hangers. Pin lighting. A pianist on weekends. Hargrove's dignity scores were dying in that beautiful room, and if you'd shown Vance the number, he'd have blamed the survey. In his mind he wasn't prejudiced — he was efficient. The word he used at staff meetings was qualified. As in: focus on qualified clients. Everyone understood the translation. Nobody wrote it down.

He read the man in the canvas jacket before the doors finished closing.

Good morning, Vance said, planting himself in the aisle — not beside the customer, in front of him, a checkpoint in a suit. Just so you're aware, our outlet location is on Fenwick Street. Great values over there. This floor starts at eight hundred dollars, and that's the socks drawer, so —

I'm here for a suit, Felix said pleasantly. Something in a charcoal. Full canvas construction, if you carry it.

Around them, Saturday commerce paused to watch. That's the thing about floors like Vance's — the staff take their cues from the top, and the customers take theirs from the staff, and within seconds the whole beautiful room had silently agreed on what the man in the jacket was worth.

The vocabulary should have registered. Full canvas is tailor's language — the difference between a suit that's built and a suit that's glued, and exactly one kind of person asks for it by name. Vance was too busy winning to hear it.

Full canvas, he repeated, with a little laugh built in for his audience. Josh Pemberton had drifted over with his commission smile, and a couple of Saturday customers had slowed to enjoy the show. Sir, our entry suit runs twenty-four hundred dollars. He let the number sit like a bouncer. Maybe let's not waste the fitting room's time today, hm?

I have time, Felix said. Do you?

Josh snorted. Vance's smile thinned into something with edges. He gestured, big and generous, toward the back corner. Then by all means. Sale rack's behind the escalator, friend. Last season. More your... he did the eye-lap, shoes to collar, pricing the jacket at nothing... speed.

Some of the customers chuckled. A silver-haired man at the tie table said, to no one and everyone, guy probably thinks it's a thrift store, and got his laugh too. That's the physics of rooms like that. Contempt is free, so everybody takes some.

By the register, a young cashier named Dominique Wells watched the whole routing operation with her jaw tight — ten months at Hargrove, long enough to have seen the trick a dozen times, junior enough that speaking up cost rent money. She caught Felix's eye for half a second. Sorry, her face said. He gave her a small nod that said noted, and she'd remember that nod later, when its full meaning arrived.

Paige Dunmore surfaced from the fragrance counter to see what was funny, decided it was the jacket, and didn't bother lowering her voice. Oh my god, she stage-whispered to Josh, is he lost? Josh said something back that made her cover her mouth. Twenty feet away, the man they were pricing kept a face his negotiating opponents knew well — pleasant, unhurried, terribly awake.

Felix went where he was pointed. Of course he did. He walked to the sale rack behind the escalator, browsing like any man with nowhere to be — while the flagship of his company demonstrated its entire disease in real time. He watched Josh intercept a young Black couple at the door and steer them, smooth as a dance move, toward accessories — steering, the file had said, and here it was, live. He watched two associates go statue-still rather than approach him. He watched Vance hold court at the suit tables, straightening lapels that didn't need it.

At the sale rack, he ran the test he ran in every store. He flagged Josh over — politely, a raised hand — and asked a real question, the kind a serious customer asks. This blazer. Is it half-canvas or fused? And what's the loro piana versus the house line, construction-wise?

Josh looked at him the way you look at a dog that has apparently spoken. Uh — it's designer, he said. It's all good stuff. Then, over Felix's shoulder, he spotted a silver watch walking in at the door, and the transformation was instant — spine, smile, voltage. Excuse me. I have a client. He left mid-sentence. He did not come back.

Felix stood with the blazer in his hands, cataloguing. Exhibit A: routing at the door. Exhibit B: product ignorance dressed as service. Exhibit C: abandonment by wallet-estimate. His father used to say you could learn everything about a shop in ten minutes if you came in wearing the wrong clothes on purpose. Marcus Thorne had never once done it on purpose.

Somewhere between the sale rack and the escalator, Felix caught his own reflection in a column mirror — a Black man in a canvas jacket, alone in ninety feet of marble — and for a strange, doubled second he saw his father on the sidewalk outside, studying the windows, loving them, staying out. Fifty years of progress, and the jacket still couldn't get past the suit tables.

Monday, he thought, is going to be a long day for some of these files.

A soft voice interrupted the arithmetic. Sir? Can I get you a water while you look? Dominique Wells had come out from behind her register to do it — crossed the whole floor, against the current, holding a bottled water like contraband. Take your time back here, she said, quiet. And if you want a fitting anyway, ask for Aldo. He'll treat you right. She was gone before he could thank her properly.

Ten months in the building, and she was the first employee to offer the new chief executive of Hargrove and Sons so much as water.

And then Felix met the one employee the dashboards had undersold.

An old man with a measuring tape around his neck came out from the alterations room, moving at the speed of craft, and began rehanging a rack the associates had disordered. His name tag said ALDO. His file said master tailor, thirty years, the last one left. He clocked Felix's jacket from ten feet — and unlike everyone else in the building, he read it correctly.

He came over without being called. May I? he asked, and at Felix's nod, took the cuff in two fingers like a jeweler. He found the elbow patch in four seconds. Looked closer. Looked up.

This is hand-felled, Aldo said quietly. This seam. Nobody does this anymore. Who made this?

My father, Felix said. In sixty-eight.

The old tailor held the cuff a moment longer, with both hands now, the way you hold something that outranks you. Your father was a serious man, he said. Wherever he learned, he learned it right. He glanced toward the suit tables, and something tired crossed his face. Don't mind the floor, sir. The floor sells labels. He tapped the cuff. Some of us still sell this.

Aldo insisted on a proper look. He walked Felix to the good fitting alcove — the one with the triple mirror, reserved by unwritten law for the suit-table clientele — and had him try the house's best charcoal, full canvas, off the rail. The old tailor circled him once, chalk in hand, murmuring measurements like a rosary. You wear clothes like a man who grew up around them, he said. Shoulders first. Most men buy the label and wear the hanger. He marked one cuff. Whoever taught you taught you the same way I was taught.

The same man who made the jacket, Felix said.

Aldo nodded slowly, as if that settled several things about the morning. Then this store, he said, should be very glad you walked in.

It was, Felix thought, the only honest transaction the flagship had offered anyone all morning.

It lasted exactly until Sterling Vance came around the escalator, saw his last master tailor chalking a full-canvas charcoal on the sale-rack customer, in the good alcove, in the triple mirror — and decided, with all eleven years of his arithmetic, that this had gone far enough.

He was right, in a way. It had. Just not in the direction he thought.

It started, as demolitions do, with the smallest brick.

Aldo, Vance said, arriving with his voice already raised for the room. Alterations has work. Actual work. Go.

The old tailor straightened up slowly. Mr. Vance, this gentleman is being fitted for the Hartfield charcoal. Full canvas. He knows cloth better than —

He's browsing the sale rack in the good alcove, Vance said, biting each word. We do not chalk suits for window shoppers. Go. Now.

Aldo looked at Felix. Felix gave him the smallest nod — go, it's all right, this is data — and the last craftsman at Hargrove and Sons folded his tape and walked back to his room with his jaw set, thirty years of swallowing it packed into forty feet of marble.

Then Vance turned to the problem in the jacket.

Who put you in the alcove? he demanded. That fitting room is for clients. Cli-ents. He said it in two syllables, a word for slow learners. You've wasted my tailor's hour, you've handled — he lifted the chalked charcoal off the rail and inspected it for damage, actually inspected it, turning it under the light — a twenty-nine-hundred-dollar garment. Are you buying this suit? Today? Cash or card, right now?
