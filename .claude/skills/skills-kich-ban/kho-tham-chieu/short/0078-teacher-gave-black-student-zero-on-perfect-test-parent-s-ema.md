Teacher Gave Black Student Zero on Perfect Test — Parent's Email Revealed Her Racism

"You gave my son a zero on a perfect test."
"And I'll give him another one if I want to. Do you think coming in here changes anything? Students like yours don't belong in my class. They never did."
"Students like mine?"
"You heard me. I don't care about your little complaints. Nobody does. Now get out of my classroom before I call security."
She didn't flinch. Didn't raise her voice. Just pulled out her phone and said five words.
"I have your email."
The teacher's face went white.
Let me tell you how we got here.
Two nights earlier. 11 p.m. Priscilla Rawlings sits at her kitchen table, checking her son's grade portal.
Zero out of a hundred.
She pulls his answer sheet from his backpack. Checks every response against the textbook. All thirty-five — perfect.
Now, Priscilla isn't just any mother. She's a civil rights attorney. She doesn't cry. She documents.
Screenshots the portal. Screenshots the answers. Opens a folder on her laptop — labeled the way she labels case files.
She emails the teacher. Professional. Measured. The response comes the next day.
"Students like yours often struggle with the rigor of my assessments. Perhaps a tutor would help."
Students like yours. Six words that changed everything.
Priscilla starts digging. She finds a complaint log at the school — fourteen entries. Every one from a Black or Brown family. Every one marked "Resolved — No Further Action."
Every one about the same teacher.
She pulls three years of grading records through a public records request. On multiple-choice tests — no racial gap. But on essays and projects, this teacher gave zeros to Black students at six times the rate of white students.
Six to one. Every semester. Three straight years.
Then Priscilla uncovers something the district buried. This teacher was disciplined at her previous school — for the exact same thing. She resigned before the hearing. Ridgemont never checked her record. Never made a single call.
The district fights back. Lawyers. A cease-and-desist letter. Anonymous posts calling Priscilla a bully. A school counselor suddenly asking about her son's "home environment."
But she doesn't stop. Because her son sat beside her one morning and whispered four words.
"Did I do something wrong?"
That's when Priscilla stopped building a case — and started building a reckoning.
The school board hearing is set. The evidence is locked. The teacher's own email — sent from her own account, verified on the district's own server — contains twelve words she can never take back.
But what happens inside that hearing room? That part — you'll have to see in Part Two.
