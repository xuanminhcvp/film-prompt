## HOOK

"Trash raises trash. Sit down."

Judge Roland Kirsch said it into the microphone at four fifteen, in Bellamy County's Courtroom Nine. He meant the woman in row two. Corliss Vann, forty-four, night-shift dispatcher. Her son sat at the defense table.

"Bailiff. Take the mother's name for the record. Spell it out loud."

"Your Honor, that isn't relevant to—"

"Sit down, counselor."

"Your Honor—"

"I said sit down."

He never looked at her.

"Ma'am. Do you work? Anywhere? And how far did you get in school? Answer both."

Forty-one people sat in the gallery. Nobody answered for her.

"Some people raise children," Kirsch said. "Some people raise defendants. Nine years up here, and I can tell which is which before either one talks."

Corliss stood. A folded bus transfer stayed flat under her thumb.

"My son has a name, Your Honor," she said. "So do I."

"Noted for the record. Next case."

He did not know what she had been before the radio room.

Four days later, he found out. It starts nine years earlier.

## THÂN TRUYỆN

Corliss Vann had worked nights for eleven years.

The shift at Bellamy County Emergency Communications ran from seven at night to six in the morning. She sat in Booth Four. Two screens, one headset, a county map she had stopped needing years ago.

A slow night was forty calls. A bad night was ninety.

She made twenty-three dollars and eleven cents an hour.

Beside her keyboard she kept a spiral notebook. Every call, she wrote three things by hand. The call number. The time. The street.

The system logged all of it already.

She wrote it down anyway.

There was a reason. Eight years back the servers went down on a Sunday and stayed down nine hours. A family on Wexler Street called three times that night. Afterward the county said there was no record of a call from that address. There was no record of anything.

Corliss had taken two of those three calls herself. She remembered the woman's voice. She could not prove she had heard it.

She bought the first notebook that Monday.

By now there were twenty-six of them in a plastic bin under her bed, rubber-banded in pairs, the dates written on the covers in marker.

At two in the morning that Monday a woman called from a house on Delaney Court and could not say what was wrong.

Corliss got her name. Then the address, twice, because the first one she gave was her mother's. Then she asked the question she always asked, which is not what happened but what do you see.

"He's on the floor. He's looking at me."

"Is he breathing?"

"I think so."

"Watch his chest and count with me. Ready. One."

The woman counted to eight and stopped and said his mouth was wrong on the left side. Corliss typed the word stroke into the field and marked the time. The unit was there in six minutes. The whole call ran four minutes and fifty seconds.

She wrote the number down. The time. The street.

Then a smoke alarm on Gable. Then a car alarm somebody was sure was a break-in. At four in the morning a man called to ask whether it was legal to sleep in a park.

"Where are you?" she asked him.

"Never mind."

"Sir. Where are you."

He hung up.

She wrote that one down too.

Nobody in that building called her anything but Vann. She had trained six of the nine people working the floor. Two of them had her cell number, for the questions they did not want to ask on a recorded line.

The morning of the ninth of August she clocked out at six and caught the Route 14 bus outside the county building. Thirty-five minutes. Six stops. She kept the transfer in her fist the whole way, because the driver on that line was strict about it and she was too tired to argue with anybody.

She was home at six forty-seven.

Tavion was already up.

He was eighteen. He had graduated in June, and he had a shift at Merrow Cold Storage that started at seven, loading pallets in a room kept at thirty-four degrees. He wore two sweatshirts to work in August.

"There's eggs," she said.

"I got it."

He ate standing at the counter, the way he had eaten since he was fourteen, one hand on the edge of the sink.

She went looking for a paper clip in the kitchen drawer, the drawer everybody has, the one with the batteries and the twist ties and the small screwdriver that fits nothing.

Under the warranty for the toaster oven there was an envelope. Cream colored. Heavier paper than an envelope needs to be. The state seal printed in the upper corner in blue.

It was sealed. So were the four behind it.

One came every October.

She closed the drawer.

"Bus or bike?" she said.

"Bike."

"Chain that thing."

"I chain it."

He kissed the side of her head on the way out, which he did not do every morning, and she stood there with the paper clip she had come for.

Then she pinned the blanket back over the bedroom window and slept until two in the afternoon. Eleven years of sleeping in the wrong half of the day, with the street coming through the wall.

Corliss grew up nine blocks east of that apartment, in a house her grandmother rented for sixty-two dollars a week.

Ruthie Vann washed linens at Saint Anselm Hospital for thirty-one years. She came home smelling like bleach and industrial steam. She did not sit down until the dishes were done. She told Corliss two things often enough that Corliss still hears them in order.

Write it down. Then keep the copy.

Ruthie died when Corliss was twenty-two. The funeral cost four thousand one hundred dollars. Corliss paid it off over nineteen months, and she has never once said that out loud to her son.

She took the dispatch job at thirty-three, after the diner closed.

Tavion's father lived in Ohio and called on birthdays.

The ice storm came through when Tavion was eleven. The east side lost power for nine days. Corliss worked six of those nights on generator, and on the fourth day she brought him with her, because there was nobody left to leave him with.

They passed a crew on Kessel. Three men in the bucket, one on the ground, working a pole that had come down across a fence.

He stood at the window of the bus and watched until the bus moved.

"How long does that take to learn," he said.

"Ask them."

He did not ask them. But that spring he started reading about it. At fifteen he could tell you the difference between a transmission line and a distribution line. At seventeen he was doing the practice test on the bus on the way to school.

He is not a talker. He thanks drivers. He carries other people's bags up the stairs in that building without being asked, which three separate neighbors have mentioned to Corliss, none of them knowing the others had.

He had never been arrested. He had been stopped twice. Both times on a bicycle.

Corliss had missed things. Eleven years of nights takes the same things from everybody: the concerts, the parent nights, the hour after school when a kid will actually tell you something. She had made two of the nine events that mattered. She kept the programs.

Rent on Wexler was nine hundred forty. On the ninth of August she had four hundred eleven dollars in savings, and she knew the number without checking, the way you know it when it is that size.

Ruthie had a rule about paper and she taught it the way you teach a child to cross a street.

Write it down. Then keep the copy.

She meant receipts, first. She meant the rent, handed over in cash on a Friday to a man who came to the door and did not always remember. She meant the hours at the hospital, which the laundry supervisor wrote in pencil.

Corliss bought Tavion his first notebook when he was twelve, a fifty-cent one with a cardboard cover, and told him the same two sentences in the same order.

He thought it was about homework.

She left school at seventeen and went to work at a diner on Halsey that stayed open until three in the morning, and she was there nine years, and the year it closed she was thirty-three and had a four-year-old.

Nine years ago, after the outage, the county held a public hearing about the emergency system. Most people who testified were engineers, and they talked about redundancy and uptime and rack capacity.

Corliss brought a notebook.

She read the call numbers out loud. The times. The streets. She read for six minutes and forty seconds and never raised her voice, and when she finished, the room was quiet in a way that county hearings usually are not.

For about a year after that her phone rang with people asking her to sit on things. Boards. Panels. Committees with long names that met on weeknights and paid mileage.

She said yes to one of them.

Then she stopped saying yes, because Tavion was nine, and because the meetings ran until ten, and because nobody was going to pay her rent for having opinions.

She went back to nights.

That is where she still was.

What she wanted now was smaller and more specific than anything a committee ever handled. It had a start date. The Halstead Line Apprenticeship took sixteen people a year out of about two hundred applications, put them on poles for four years, and paid them while it did it. Journeyman linemen in the county cleared eighty-one thousand dollars.

Tavion tested in at number eleven.

The letter said orientation was the fourteenth of September, and it listed what he needed to bring, and one line near the bottom said what he needed not to have. No criminal conviction in the preceding three years.

He bought the gloves in July, before he was even sure. Forty-seven dollars, class two, yellow, and he kept them in the box on the shelf above his bed and did not wear them once.

Judge Roland Kirsch wanted something too.

He was fifty-eight. Twelve years on the county bench. In the last week of July the appellate appointments came out of the governor's office, four names, and his was not among them.

It was the second time.

His clerk noticed that his morning calendars got faster after that. Nineteen matters between nine and eleven, some of them under ninety seconds. The clerk mentioned it to nobody, because clerks do not.

Tavion bought the bicycle on a Saturday, in the parking lot behind the hardware store on Kessel Avenue, from a man he had never seen before and did not see again.

Twenty dollars. A gray Trek with a bent fender and no serial plate.

He asked for a receipt because his mother had asked him, twice, whether he had gotten a receipt for anything he ever bought secondhand. The man tore a page out of a spiral pad and wrote bike, twenty dollars, and signed something that was not a name so much as a shape.

Tavion folded it into his wallet.

Eleven days later a patrol officer stopped him on Kessel at five in the morning because the bicycle matched a description in a report filed in June. He was cooperative. He gave his name. He took the receipt out of his wallet and held it out, and the officer looked at it and did not take it.

Possession of stolen property. A misdemeanor.

The arraignment was set for the following Tuesday at nine.

Corliss worked that Monday night. She took fifty-two calls, including a stroke on Delaney Court and a kitchen fire that turned out to be a pan. She clocked out at six. She did not go home.

She caught the Route 14 downtown instead. By seven twenty she was in the courthouse hallway, still in the jacket she had worked in. County patch on the shoulder. Her last name stitched over the pocket in white thread.

The hallway outside Courtroom Nine holds about sixty people. There were more than that.

A woman with a toddler asleep on her shoulder. Two men in work boots who had taken the morning off and kept checking the time. A girl of maybe nineteen practicing something under her breath.

The nine o'clock list had thirty-one names on it.

Corliss found a place along the wall and stayed there. She had been awake since six the evening before.

She did not know the judge's name that morning. If anybody had asked, she would have said she had never heard it.

At eight thirty Kirsch was in chambers with the door open. His clerk brought him the calendar and he looked at the length of it.

"Thirty-one," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"We'll be done by eleven."

He said it the way a man says a thing he intends to make true. Then he put on the robe, which takes about eight seconds and changes what a room does when you walk into it.

He had been on this bench twelve years. He was good at the part of the job that is scheduling. Among the public defenders he had a reputation as fast, which is the word they use when they cannot use a worse one out loud.

The line moved into Courtroom Nine slowly because the metal detector at that end of the hall has one operator.

Ahead of Corliss, a man in a windbreaker asked which courtroom, and somebody told him, and he made a face.

"Nine," he said. "Good luck."

Stroud heard it. Stroud hears it every Tuesday. There is a thing public defenders do in that building. They tell a client what to expect without telling them what they think. It takes about four sentences. Stroud had spent two of his seven minutes on it.

"Don't explain," he had told Tavion. "Answer what he asks. If he asks something that isn't about the case, look at me."

"Why would he ask something that isn't about the case?"

"He probably won't," Stroud said.

Emil Stroud met Tavion at eight forty.

Stroud had nineteen files that morning. He had read this one in the elevator. He was decent, and he was fast, and he had seven minutes.

"They'll offer diversion," he said. "First offense, no record, you're eighteen by six weeks. You do the program, it goes away."

"And the apprenticeship?"

"If it goes away, there's nothing to report."

Tavion nodded.

Stroud looked at the receipt for the first time and said it was good, they should put it in, it was worth something.

At nine o'clock the doors of Courtroom Nine opened and forty-one people went in and sat down, and Corliss took a seat in the second row because the first row was already full.

Tavion was number twenty-two on the list.

That meant waiting. A domestic case, a suspended license, three failures to appear, a man who had cut through a fence to get to his own truck. Kirsch moved them. Ninety seconds. Two minutes. Four.

Before Tavion, there was a woman in her sixties who had missed two payments on a fine from a case that ended in April.

"Do you have it today?"

"I have forty."

"The clerk's window is down the hall. Next."

Then a man whose license had been suspended for a lapse in insurance he had already fixed, with the paperwork, which he held up.

"That's a matter for the department, not for me. Next."

That was the rhythm of the room by ten. Not cruel. Just moving. People were getting nine or ten seconds each and most of them had taken a day off work for it.

Corliss watched all of it from the second row and thought, without any special feeling about it, that the man was tired and behind and that her son's file was thin and clean and would take two minutes.

At ten twenty he called the number.

Tavion stood up. Stroud stood up beside him and gave the case number and his own name for the record, and the assistant district attorney, a man named Nolan Grieves, gave his.

"Misdemeanor possession of stolen property," Grieves said. "The state's recommending diversion, Your Honor. No record, he's eighteen by six weeks, and there's a receipt."

"A receipt."

"He bought it in a parking lot for twenty dollars. He asked for a receipt."

Stroud held it up. A page torn from a spiral pad, folded in four, soft at the creases from eleven days in a wallet.

Nobody took it from him.

"Your Honor, the state has no objection," Grieves said. "We'd ask the court to approve."

Kirsch was reading something else.

"Denied."

Stroud waited a second. "Your Honor—"

"I said denied. Diversion is for people who are going somewhere. Put it on the record that the court finds this defendant is not a candidate."

"On what basis," Stroud said.

That was the wrong question, or the right one, and either way it opened a door.

Kirsch looked up for the first time. Not at Tavion. Past him, at the second row, where a woman in a county jacket had been sitting since seven twenty in the morning with her hands in her lap.

"On the basis of what walks in here every Tuesday," he said. "Trash raises trash. Sit down."

Tavion turned his head.

That is all he did. He was facing the bench and he turned it maybe thirty degrees toward the gallery, toward the second row, and Stroud put two fingers on his forearm and left them there.

"Eyes front," Stroud said, very quietly, not moving his mouth much.

Grieves looked at the table.

The court reporter's hands kept moving. That is the job.

Corliss did not look at her son. She had worked out in about a second and a half that if she looked at him he would say something, and that whatever he said would go in the file under his name and not hers.

So she looked at the seal on the wall behind the bench and kept her hands where they were.

Forty-one people were in that gallery. A woman near the aisle with a toddler asleep on her shoulder opened her mouth. She had a case at eleven. She closed it.

"Bailiff. Take the mother's name for the record. Spell it out loud."

"Your Honor, that isn't relevant to—"

"Sit down, counselor."

"Your Honor—"

"I said sit down."

He never looked at her.

"Ma'am. Do you work? Anywhere? And how far did you get in school? Answer both."

One of the men in work boots leaned forward. His neighbor put a hand on his arm, and he sat back, because he had a hearing of his own at nine forty and had already lost the morning.

Nobody answered for her.

The silence in that room lasted about seven seconds, which is long enough for everyone in it to understand that nobody is going to fill it.

A woman in the last row stood up and went out. The door at the back of Courtroom Nine has a closer on it that does not work, so it made the sound it makes.

The bailiff came down the side aisle to the end of the second row with a pad in his hand. He was maybe twenty-six. He did not look like he wanted the pad.

"Ma'am. Last name."

"Vann."

"Spell it, please."

"V, A, double N."

He wrote it. Then he looked at what he had written, crossed something out, and wrote it again.

"First name?"

"Corliss."

"Ma'am, he's going to ask me the other one too."

She told him where she worked. He wrote that down as well, and said thank you, quietly, in the tone people use when they are doing something they will think about that night.

Then he walked back up the aisle and handed the pad to the clerk, and the clerk entered it, because it had been ordered on the record and a clerk does not get to decide which orders are worth entering.

Corliss watched her own name go into a machine.

She had put her name on things before. She had signed a lease, and a funeral bill, and the back of eleven years of paychecks. She had signed one other kind of document, a long time ago, four times in one afternoon, and had not thought about any of them since.

There was a sentence Corliss could have said in that room. It was eleven words long. She had it in her mouth.

She also had a son standing eight feet away in front of a man who set his bail, his fine, his terms and his record, and who had just been told in open court that he was wrong about something.

She counted what the sentence would cost before she was halfway through it.

"Some people raise children," Kirsch said. "Some people raise defendants. Nine years up here, and I can tell which is which before either one talks."

Corliss stood.

The bus transfer stayed flat under her thumb.

"My son has a name, Your Honor," she said. "So do I."

"Noted for the record. Next case."

The gavel is not loud. People think it is. It is a wooden sound that lasts less than a second and it ends things.

Tavion took the plea because Stroud told him what a trial costs and how long it takes and what it does to a September start date. Sixty days suspended. A hundred fifty dollar fine. A hundred ninety in fees.

And a conviction.

They gave the bicycle back to a man on Farley Street who had reported it in June and who, when the property clerk called him, asked whether he really had to come get it.

Tavion called the apprenticeship office from the pay lot outside the courthouse at eleven fifty, before his mother could tell him to wait a day.

The coordinator's name was Lorna Whitcomb. She had a good voice for the job. She listened to all of it.

"When was the conviction entered?"

"An hour ago."

"Okay," she said. "Okay. I want to be straight with you, because I'd rather you hear it from me than from a letter. The three-year clause isn't ours. It's in the safety agreement with the utility. I can't waive it and neither can my director."

"It was a bicycle."

"I believe you."

"I have the receipt."

"I believe you," she said again, and then she was quiet for a moment. "You can reapply for the class after next. That's twenty months."

He said thank you. He said it the way he thanks drivers.

Then he stood in the pay lot for a while with the phone in his hand.

The gloves were forty-seven dollars and the return window was thirty days and he had bought them on the eleventh of July.

He went to the store anyway on the way home, because when you are eighteen you go. A kid at the counter with a lanyard explained the policy twice, politely. He offered store credit. Tavion did not take it.

Corliss got home at one.

She did not go up right away. She sat on the third step of the front stoop, in the jacket, in the middle of the day, on a street where everybody who lives there was at work.

A neighbor came out and asked if she was all right and she said she was fine, she was just off shift.

Upstairs, Tavion had the box on the kitchen table.

"You stood up," he said.

"I did."

"He put your name in the record before you stood up."

"I know."

"Then what was it for."

She did not have an answer that fit in the room.

"Ma," he said, and his voice did the thing it had done since he was small when he was trying to be reasonable in a situation that did not deserve it. "I don't need anybody to say something. I needed the class."

He put the box on the shelf above his bed, where it had been, and did not open it.

That night she called out of work for the first time in four years.

She sat at the kitchen table until the light went blue and then gray, with the notebooks out of the bin, twenty-six of them stacked in two towers.

She was not reading them.

She was looking at what they were.

At Merrow, they found out on Wednesday, because the application he had signed in June had a box on it and the box has to stay true.

He told his shift lead himself, before anybody asked.

The shift lead was decent about it and took it to the office, and the office took four days, and for four days a kid who loads pallets at thirty-four degrees did not know if he had a job either.

They kept him. The manager said something about it being a bicycle, and said it in a tone that made clear the decision had been closer than it should have been.

He came home that Friday and told his mother he still had the job, and she said good, and he said it like it was good news, and they both worked at that for about a minute.

Then he went to bed at eight because he had a five o'clock start.

Corliss sat up.

Later that week she noticed she had stopped writing things down.

Four days, no notebook. She had worked two shifts in that stretch and logged everything into the system like everybody else and gone home.

On Thursday morning she stood in the doorway of her son's room before he was up.

The box was on the shelf. He had turned it so the label faced the wall, which was the only thing about the whole week that she could not look at directly.

She went to the kitchen and got a notebook out of the bin and put it on the table and sat down in front of it.

She wrote the date.

Then she wrote what had been said in that courtroom, as much of it as she could remember, and got about a third of it, and stopped, because a third is not a record. A third is a story about a record.

That was Thursday. She went in and worked Thursday night and Friday night, and on Saturday she slept until two, and on Sunday she did the thing she had been circling since the gavel.

She figured out what a person is allowed to buy.

The clerk's office opens at eight thirty.

Corliss was there at eight twenty on Wednesday morning with a form she had already filled out, because she had looked up which form it was the night before.

"Audio of a hearing?"

"Courtroom Nine. Tuesday. Nine o'clock calendar, number twenty-two."

"You a party to the case?"

"I'm his mother."

"That's not a party." The woman behind the glass said it the way you say a thing forty times a week. "But it's a public proceeding, so it doesn't matter. Forty-two dollars."

Corliss paid it in cash, in tens, and got a receipt.

She went home and put the file on the laptop Tavion used for school and listened to the whole thing with a new notebook open in front of her.

Then she wrote it down.

Not summarized. Written. Every word, in her hand, the way she had written call numbers for eight years. Including the parts that were nothing. Including a cough from the gallery. Including the four seconds of silence after her son's lawyer asked on what basis.

She went back over one stretch four times to be sure of the order. He had said the sentence about trash before the bailiff took her name, not after. That mattered to her, though she could not have told you why yet.

It took her until two in the afternoon.

At the bottom of the last page she wrote the date and the words she had written on the cover of twenty-six notebooks. Then she made a copy at the drugstore on the corner for eleven cents a page.

Now she needed to know where a thing like this goes.

She did not know. She had never needed to know. And the county website had a page for filing a complaint about a clerk, and a page for filing a complaint about a deputy, and no page that said what to do about the man in the robe.

She stood in the kitchen with her hand on the drawer for a while.

Then she opened it, and took out the envelope with the state seal in the corner, and opened that too, for the first time since they started coming.

It was a newsletter. Twelve pages, stapled, the kind an office produces because somebody's job description says to. Names of people who had retired. A photograph of a building. On the inside of the back cover, a list of addresses, and the sixth one down was the State Commission on Judicial Conduct.

She wrote the address on her hand, which is a thing she has done since she was nineteen.

The complaint was three pages long, in pen.

She did not ask for anybody to be removed. She read what she had written twice and took out the one sentence that came close, because it was the only sentence in three pages that was about him instead of about the record.

What she asked for was two things, numbered.

That the case be reopened and reheard.

That the reason given in open court on the tenth of August be entered in the file, in full, in the words that were used.

She mailed the original to the capital on Thursday morning, certified, eight dollars and forty-two cents. She walked a copy across to the county courthouse and left it at the window of the presiding judge's chambers on the fourth floor. The thing she actually wanted done was reopening her son's case, and the capital could not do that.

The window clerk stamped it and put it in a tray.

Presiding Judge Delia Fontaine read it Friday morning.

She read it once fast and once slowly. Then she did what the manual says to do with a complaint that names a sitting judge, which is to verify that the complainant is who the complainant says she is before anything else happens to anybody.

She called down to records.

Odette Prewitt had been in that basement twenty-two years. She could find a file from before the system without using the system, and she was one of four people in the building who knew where the appointment binders were kept, because nobody asks for the appointment binders.

She ran the name.

It came back twice.

The first was the one everybody expected. Vann, Corliss R., listed as present in the gallery, mother of the defendant, case number and date, tenth of August.

The second was a records identifier with a prefix Prewitt had not seen in a while.

She went and got the binder.

The Ninth District Judicial Nominating Commission had nine members. Five lawyers, four citizens. The citizens were appointed by the governor's office for one four-year term, they were paid mileage, and they were the only people in the process who had never worked inside a courthouse in their lives.

The binder held the certificate of nomination for every judge the commission had sent forward.

Prewitt turned to the tab that said Kirsch, R., and there were three pages, and on the second one, at the bottom, in a column of nine signatures, was a name written in the same hand as the complaint upstairs.

Corliss R. Vann. Citizen member. The twelfth of March, nine years earlier.

Stapled behind it were the panel minutes, because minutes go in the binder, and the minutes are not sealed after five years.

Prewitt read a paragraph of them standing up.

Then she carried the whole binder to the fourth floor herself instead of sending it, which she had not done in some years.

Judge Fontaine put the two documents side by side on her desk.

She looked at them for longer than she needed to.

Then she asked her clerk to find out whether Ms. Vann could come in on Monday, and to put Judge Kirsch on her calendar for four o'clock, and to hold the rest of the afternoon.

Fontaine sat with the binder open for most of an hour before she called anybody.

She had been on the bench nineteen years. She had presided over the county for four. In that time she had sent two complaints up to the capital, and both had come back with a letter and nothing else, and she had learned what that felt like.

This one had a document attached to it that she had never seen attached to anything.

She checked the minutes against the certificate twice. Then she called the capital herself, and confirmed that the original had arrived Friday, and told them the county would be reopening the underlying case regardless of what they did.

Then she asked her clerk to make the two calls.

Corliss came in the jacket. She had thought about not wearing it and had put it on anyway.

They let her sit in the outer office for eleven minutes.

There is a brass plate on that door with a name on it and a smaller line underneath. She read it twice. The carpet in that hallway is the same carpet as the third floor, which she had noticed on Thursday and noticed again.

A clerk asked if she wanted coffee. She said no thank you.

Then she took the notebook out of her bag and wrote the date, and the floor, and the time she had arrived, and the time it was now.

Eleven minutes is not a long wait. She wrote both numbers anyway.

At four ten, Kirsch came up the back stairs the way judges do, and he saw a woman on the bench outside the presiding judge's office, and he did not recognize her, and he went in.

Fontaine did not ask him to sit.

"There's a complaint," she said. "It's filed at the capital. There's a copy here because she's also asking me to reopen the case, which I'm going to do."

"Which case."

"The tenth. Nine o'clock calendar, number twenty-two."

"I don't remember it."

"You will." She turned the first page toward him. "That's the transcript she made from the audio. It's handwritten. Ours will say the same thing, because I pulled ours this morning."

He read it standing up. He got to the fourth line and stopped and read it again.

"That's out of context."

"It's three sentences of context on either side, and I have the file, and the state's attorney had recommended diversion." She let that sit. "That's not why you're here."

"Then why am I here."

She turned the binder around.

It was open to a page with nine signatures on it and his own name printed across the top in a typeface nobody uses anymore.

He looked at it the way you look at a word you have read a thousand times, waiting for it to mean what it always meant.

Then he put one hand flat on the edge of the desk.

He did not say anything for eight seconds.

"That's a different person," he said.

"It isn't."

"There were nine of them. I was in that room for forty minutes. I don't—"

"Roland."

He stopped.

Fontaine turned the page and put her finger halfway down the minutes and left it there while he read.

The paragraph said that on a motion to remove the applicant Kirsch from the short list, the vote had been four to four. It said that Commissioner Vann had asked the panel for one more question. It recorded what she asked, and it recorded that after his answer, one commissioner changed a vote.

It said his name went forward five to four.

The chair in that room is not comfortable. He sat down in it anyway.

Fontaine went to the door and opened it.

Corliss stood up out of the hall bench, and came in, and stood where she was put, and she was tired in the specific way of a woman who had worked eleven of the last fourteen nights.

Kirsch got up. That was reflex; you get up.

"Ms. Vann," Fontaine said. "You've read your complaint back to me. He's read it too."

He looked at her for a while.

"I don't remember you," he said.

"Neither did I," Corliss said. "Until Tuesday."

That was all of it.

Then she reached across the desk, turned her own complaint around so it faced her, and put two fingers on the page beside the numbered lines.

"One and two," she said. "That's what I'm asking for. Nothing under it."

Fontaine said the case would be reheard Thursday in front of another judge.

Kirsch said he wanted a moment, and did not get one, because the moment had already happened and everyone in the room had been present for it.

The case was reheard on Thursday the twenty-first, at nine, in Courtroom Four, in front of Judge Ines Marchetti.

It took nine minutes.

Grieves recommended diversion again. Marchetti asked to see the receipt, and read it, and asked Tavion two questions about where the parking lot was and what time of day it had been. Then she approved the program, vacated the plea, and set a review date in six months.

"Mr. Vann," she said. "Do you understand that when you finish, there is no conviction?"

"Yes, ma'am."

The whole thing was over before the ten o'clock calendar.

He had to take the box down and turn it around to check what size he had bought.

The audio and the transcript went into the case file, and a case file is public.

A reporter who covers the county courts pulled it in the last week of August because a clerk mentioned that a presiding judge had reopened a misdemeanor, which does not happen. The story ran on a Sunday. It ran with three sentences quoted in full and the rest of the calendar's timing laid out in a table: thirty-one matters, two hours, the average length of each one.

Four more complaints reached the capital within eleven days.

One was from a man who had been told in that courtroom in March that he should be embarrassed to bring his family in. One was from a woman who had been asked her immigration status in a rent dispute where nobody's status was at issue. Two were from lawyers, and lawyers do not file those, because they have to come back Tuesday.

The State Commission on Judicial Conduct opened a review and pulled fourteen months of recordings.

It moved the way those things move, which is slowly. There was a hearing in February. Kirsch was represented, and his lawyer argued that the docket pressure in that division was the real subject, and that argument was not entirely wrong, and it did not save him.

The commission issued a public censure in April.

He was not removed. Almost nobody is. But a censure goes in the file, and the file is the file the appellate nominating commission reads, and there is no version of that room where they get past page one.

He filed his retirement papers in March of the following year, eleven months early.

Corliss was invited to testify in February and did not go.

She sent the notebook instead — the copy, not the original — with a cover letter of four sentences saying that everything she had to say was already written down in the order it happened, and that she worked nights.

Bellamy County changed two things that year, and neither one had her name on it.

Recordings of misdemeanor calendars are now kept for three years instead of one. And there is a laminated card taped inside the door of every courtroom in that building, at eye level. Eleven lines. It says who a judge may question, and on what subject. One of those lines says that a person seated in the gallery is not a party to a proceeding.

Nobody put a plaque up. Nobody called anybody a hero. A maintenance man taped up nineteen cards in one afternoon and went to lunch.

Tavion works a bucket now, in his second year.

He is on a crew of four. He still does not talk much, and his foreman has told him twice that this is a good quality in a man who works around energized line. He makes twenty-six dollars and forty cents an hour as a second-year, which is three dollars and change more than his mother has ever made, and neither of them has said that out loud either.

Corliss still works nights. She has been asked twice to test for the supervisor's desk, which is days, and she has not, and when Tavion asked her why, she said the desk does not take calls.

She still writes them down. There are thirty-one notebooks in the bin now.

The one from that August is not in the bin. It is in the kitchen drawer, under the warranty for the toaster oven, with a cream-colored envelope that has been opened.

On the morning of the fourteenth of September, orientation day, she clocked out at six and walked to the stop outside the county building. He was already there. His class started at seven thirty, and this was the bus that got him there.

They stood next to each other under the shelter.

It was still dark. He had the gloves in a bag. He had a lunch she had made at four in the morning during a slow stretch. And he was doing the thing with his jaw that he does when he is early for something that matters.

The bus came. She went through her pockets on the step and could not find her transfer.

"Here," he said.

He put his in her hand, folded in four, soft at the creases.

"You need that."

"I got another one."

He didn't. He paid the fare again, in coins, standing behind her, and neither of them said anything about it, and the driver on that line is strict about transfers but he was not strict that morning.

She sat down by the window.

He stood in the aisle beside her seat with one hand on the rail, going the same direction she was going, for four stops, until she got off at Wexler and he kept on downtown.
