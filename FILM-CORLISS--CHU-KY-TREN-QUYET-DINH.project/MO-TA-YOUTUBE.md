🎬 Judge Called a Black Teen's Mother Trash in Open Court — Her Signature Was on His Appointment

Trash raises trash. Sit down. Judge Roland Kirsch says it into the microphone at four fifteen on a Tuesday, in Courtroom Nine of the Bellamy County Courthouse, and he is not talking to the defendant. He is talking about the woman in row two. Corliss Vann is forty-four. She has been awake since six the evening before, she is still in the county jacket she works in, and her son is standing eight feet away for a bicycle he bought in a parking lot for twenty dollars with a receipt in his wallet. The state recommended diversion. The judge denied it. Then he orders the bailiff to take her name for the record and spell it out loud, and forty-one people watch a woman read her own name into a machine. Nobody answers for her. Four days later, a woman he has never seen puts two pieces of paper on a desk in front of him, and one of them has that same name at the bottom of it.

What he doesn't know — what he understands in about eight seconds without being able to say it? Nine years earlier, Corliss Vann sat one four-year term as a citizen member of the Ninth District Judicial Nominating Commission. Five lawyers, four ordinary people, mileage only. The motion to strike his application died four to four. She asked the panel for one more question. After his answer, a commissioner changed a vote, and his name went forward five to four, and her signature is on the certificate dated the twelfth of March. She did not remember him either. She had not opened the envelopes the state mails her every October. And she does not ask for his job — she asks for two numbered lines, and the system takes eleven months to do the rest.
