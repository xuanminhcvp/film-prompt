That was the crisis in Deacon Boyle's life. Not the title defense in November. The algorithm.
Understand the champion before you judge the man. Twenty-six years old, twenty-four wins, no losses, hands genuinely touched by God — even people who hated him said so. He'd come up hard out of a car-less, father-quiet childhood, saved by a boxing gym the way some men are saved by religion, and then, between the belt and the blue checkmark, converted to a different faith entirely. The content faith. Boxing paid him six figures a fight. The phone paid him more, and the phone was hungrier.
His gym — Ironline Boxing, a converted warehouse with good light and honest smells — had turned into a studio. Camera guys outnumbered corner men. His hype man, Rico Delgado, ran the channel: prank challenges, trash-talk clips, and the reliable classic, the ten-thousand-dollar dare. Ten grand to any civilian who could land one clean shot on the champ. Delivery drivers swinging wild while Deacon slipped and mugged for the lens. Weekend warriors gassing out in forty seconds. The clips did numbers. The numbers did something to Deacon.
And in the corner of every frame, wearing the expression of a man watching his church get turned into a casino, stood Teddy Marsh.
